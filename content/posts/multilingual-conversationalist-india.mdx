A collections agent calls a borrower about an overdue EMI. It opens in Hindi.

> **Agent (Hindi):** नमस्ते, मैं बोलना से बोल रहा हूँ। आपकी EMI ड्यू है।\
> _Hello, I'm calling from Bolna. Your EMI is due._
>
> **Customer:** हाँ हाँ… मला माहीत आहे, पण पगार उद्या येतोय.\
> _Yes, yes… I know, but my salary comes in tomorrow._

The first two words are Hindi. The rest is Marathi. The borrower answered in the language they thought the caller wanted, then switched to their own as soon as the call reached what mattered: when they can pay.

The agent switched too.

> **Agent (Marathi):** काही हरकत नाही. उद्या पेमेंट लिंक पाठवतो, चालेल का?\
> _No problem. I'll send the payment link tomorrow. Does that work?_

The call ended with a date and a payment link. If the agent had stayed in Hindi, the borrower would have had to explain their salary date in a language they speak less easily. Most people don't. They say "theek hai" and hang up.

The same moment shows up in other industries. On an insurance renewal call in Bengaluru, a customer answers in English and then says in Kannada that this month is difficult. An English-only agent misses the one thing that mattered. On a hospital reminder in Kolkata, a son picks up and hands the phone to his mother, who speaks Bengali. The agent has to follow her, not him.

On Bolna last month, about one in eight calls with live language detection switched language at least once.

## Why language keeps changing in India

In 2011, the Census asked every resident for their mother tongue. It got back 19,569 different answers, which were grouped into 121 languages. Twenty-two of these are listed in the Constitution.

Even these numbers simplify things. "Hindi" in the Census covers more than fifty mother tongues, including Bhojpuri, which alone has about 5 crore speakers. States are also less uniform than a map suggests. A third of Karnataka grew up speaking something other than Kannada. Assam is about half Assamese and nearly a third Bengali.

![Map of India showing each state's largest mother tongue in the 2011 Census, shaded by how much of the state speaks it: Hindi across the north at 78 to 94%, Marathi 69% in Maharashtra, Kannada 66% in Karnataka, Assamese 48% in Assam, Bengali 86% in West Bengal, Malayalam 97% in Kerala and Tamil 88% in Tamil Nadu](/images/posts/multilingual-conversationalist-india/largest-mother-tongue-by-state.png)

More than a quarter of Indians also speak a second language, and people pick a language based on who they are talking to. When a call comes from an unknown number, most people guess Hindi or English. That is why the same pattern keeps showing up on calls:

-   Callers open in the language they think you expect, then move to their own.
-   The important part, like an objection, a salary date or a promise to pay, comes after that move.
-   If the agent replies in the wrong language, the caller stops explaining and soon hangs up.

## Three ways voice agents fail

Good AI voices already exist for many Indian languages. The hard part is deciding which language to use, and when.

**Stuck in one language.** The agent is set up in Hindi and stays in Hindi. Customer records can suggest a language, but the person who answers may not match the record.

**Switching too late.** Some agents switch only when the caller asks, or only after several turns. The customer hears at least one reply in the wrong language, usually right after sharing something personal.

**One model for every language.** Speech recognition quality varies a lot from one language to the next. In AI4Bharat's Vistaar benchmark, models built for Indian languages got about 1 in 7 Hindi words wrong, but nearly 1 in 3 Malayalam words. Noisy phone lines make it worse.

![Bar chart of IndicWhisper's average word error rate across the Vistaar benchmarks: Hindi 13.6%, Marathi 18.2%, Kannada 18.3%, Urdu 19.4%, Bengali 20.1%, Punjabi 20.5%, Gujarati 22.8%, Tamil 25.3%, Odia 27.4%, Telugu 28.8% and Malayalam 32.3%](/images/posts/multilingual-conversationalist-india/vistaar-word-error-rate.png)

So on Bolna, each language on an agent gets its own speech recognition and voice model, for example Sarvam for Hindi and Deepgram for English. When a better Tamil model comes out, it goes into the Tamil slot and the rest of the agent stays the same.

**Tip:** when comparing vendors, ask for one recorded call that switches language twice.

## How LiD works on Bolna

Language identification (LiD) on Bolna has two parts.

![Diagram of how a Bolna agent decides which language to speak: the caller's speech goes to the Detector, a main recognizer locked to the current language and a background detector that assumes no language, then to the Switch, which ignores fillers, borrowed words and numbers. If the caller has moved to another configured language, the transcriber, voice and prompt all move to it; if not, the agent stays put](/images/posts/multilingual-conversationalist-india/how-lid-decides.png)

**The Detector.** Two listeners run during the call. One is set to the language the agent is currently speaking. The other writes down whatever it hears, without assuming any language.

**The Switch.** At the end of each caller turn, before the agent replies, the Switch asks one question: has the caller moved to another language this agent supports? It is careful by design. It switches when the caller speaks a full sentence in another language, asks for a language, or keeps answering in one. It ignores fillers like "haan" and "okay", English words like "EMI" inside a Marathi sentence, and numbers such as OTPs or account IDs.

When it switches, the whole agent moves together. Speech recognition, voice and the agent's instructions all change to the new language, and the agent can introduce itself in that language. The switch adds a pause of a few hundred milliseconds, only on that one turn.

On the opening call, the Switch treats "हाँ हाँ" as a filler. Hindi and Marathi are close, so it looks at the grammar of the whole sentence. "मला माहीत आहे" is Marathi; in Hindi it would be "मुझे पता है". So the Switch moves to Marathi, and the next reply is in Marathi.

Teams that want more control can set an agent to switch only when the caller asks.

## Why speed matters

In everyday conversation, people leave a gap of only about 200 milliseconds between turns. Research across ten languages, from Japanese to languages spoken in Mexico and Papua New Guinea, found people everywhere keep this gap short.

A caller who has just switched to Marathi expects the next reply right away, and in Marathi. An agent that sends one more Hindi reply while it works things out breaks that rhythm. That is why Bolna makes the decision before the reply is written.

## Where it helps

Language identification helps on any call where the useful part is personal:

-   **Lending:** EMI reminders and collections, where salary dates and payment promises come up.
-   **Sales and lead qualification:** objections about fees, income or eligibility.
-   **KYC and insurance renewals:** why a document is missing or a payment is late.
-   **Healthcare reminders:** a family member often takes the phone.
-   **Deliveries:** confirming a new time slot or address.
-   **Support lines:** callers describe problems best in their own language.

## The choice of caller

Nobody on the opening call asked for Marathi. The borrower simply switched, the way people across India do many times a day. A good agent notices that early and follows on the very next turn, so the customer never has to ask.
